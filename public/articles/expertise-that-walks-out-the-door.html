The expertise that walks out the door

An organisation's most valuable knowledge is usually the least written down. Encoding how experts actually execute — not just what they produce — is how you keep it.

Published
July 2026
Reading time
5 minutes
Topic
Encoding tacit knowledge

Ask an organisation where its critical knowledge lives and it will point you at a document library. The honest answer is that most of it lives in a handful of people, and it leaves when they do.

Every organisation has them: the person who knows which exception to watch for, the one whose sign-off everyone quietly waits on, the analyst who can tell at a glance that a number is wrong. Their expertise is real, it is load-bearing, and it is almost entirely undocumented. It survives on habit and proximity — until a resignation, a reorganisation, or a retirement takes it out of the building for good.

What gets written down is the output: the policy, the procedure, the finished report. What doesn't get written down is the reasoning that produces it reliably under real conditions — the attention, the judgement, the recovery when something goes wrong. That reasoning is the asset. It is also the part that walks out the door.

Procedures capture the what, not the how

A procedure tells you the steps. It rarely tells you what to be watching for at step three, what a good result looks like versus a merely finished one, or what to do when an input arrives malformed. Those judgements are exactly what separates an expert from a checklist-follower, and they are precisely what a procedure leaves out.

This is why "we have it documented" so often fails in practice. The document is a description of the happy path. The expertise is everything the happy path assumes and never states — and when the person holding it leaves, the description turns out to describe very little.

Most organisations hold their critical execution knowledge in people's heads, in undocumented habits, and in expertise that walks out the door.

Encoding execution, not just describing it

The alternative is to treat expert execution as something that can be specified. Not narrated in prose, but structured: the task broken into units with typed inputs and outputs; the attention made explicit — what to focus on, when, and what triggers a shift; tolerances stated rather than assumed, so deviation is measured instead of guessed; and recovery paths written down in advance, so "what if it goes wrong" has an answer before it does.

This is the thinking behind PULSE, our framework for encoding reliable task execution. Its purpose is to capture the tacit reasoning behind expert work and turn it into an asset the organisation owns — one that is both human-readable and machine-consumable. It does not replace policy; it operationalises it, and it replaces the procedure documentation that was never quite enough.

Why this matters more now, not less

It is tempting to assume AI makes this obsolete — that models will simply absorb the expertise. The opposite is true. An AI agent is only as good as the specification of the work you hand it. Vague instructions produce confident, plausible, wrong results. The organisations getting real value from automation are the ones that have done the unglamorous work of making their expertise explicit enough to delegate — to a person or a machine.

Encoded expertise is what lets you allocate work deliberately: this step to a person, this one to a tool, this one to a governed combination — grounded in what each can actually do, and at what quality threshold. Without it, "human-machine allocation" is a slide. With it, it is an operating model.

Where to start

Not with everything. Start with the process that would hurt most if the key person left tomorrow. Sit with how the work is really done, exceptions and all. Capture the judgement, not just the sequence. You will usually find that the act of making it explicit improves the process on its own — ambiguity surfaced is ambiguity that can be fixed.

The knowledge was always an asset. Encoding it is what turns it into one you keep.
