Business architecture and the roadmap that survives

Roadmap failures are much more often sequencing failures than selection failures.

Published
July 2026
Reading time
8 minutes
Topic
Business architecture & roadmaps

Think about the last greenfield or complex programme kick-off you attended. There were probably three groups in the room, and they were not really in the same conversation.

The business people want to reach more customers and generate more revenue, and they want it today. The technology team want to build something that will still be standing in five years, and preferably something interesting to build. Operations and the back office want to stop fielding calls from customers asking for things the current process cannot do without a workaround. Every one of those objectives is correct. Every one of them needs addressing. None of them is a plan.

So how does the group get from three legitimate positions to a single coherent target?

Traditionally, whoever is loudest or most senior gets the say. It is easy to read that as politics, and sometimes it is, but that is not the whole story. The honest reason is that there is usually nothing on the screen to point at. The team has often been ramped up in the past few months. Half the people in the room have never seen the end-to-end process work in anger and do not know where it breaks. When there is no shared picture of how the organisation actually operates, the only available tiebreaker is confidence, and confidence is distributed unevenly.

Two things follow from that room, and both of them are expensive.

The order is wrong, not the list

Most organisations can name the right initiatives. Ask an executive team what needs to change and you will generally get a sensible list. What goes wrong is the sequence. Initiative four depends on a capability that initiative seven was going to build. Something ships into a part of the business that cannot absorb it because the process and the people around it have not moved yet. Work stalls waiting on a dependency nobody mapped, and by the time it clears, the assumptions it was built on have changed.

Roadmap failures are much more often sequencing failures than selection failures. Sequencing cannot be reasoned about without a view of what has to exist, and at what standard, before the next thing delivers any value at all.

Take document generation from templates. Sales want a proposal to go out at the click of a button so they can quote on the call. Operations want a confirmation letter to fire automatically when a customer changes an address, so the team stops producing them by hand. Compliance want every outbound template held in one place, versioned, with approval recorded. Three initiatives, three sponsors, three business cases, and one capability sitting underneath all of them.

Run separately, sales buys something bolted to the CRM, operations scripts a workaround off the servicing system, and compliance ends up policing two template sets it does not control. Sequenced against the capability, the template store and the approval route are built once and first, and the two front-end initiatives become much smaller pieces of work on top of it. The list of initiatives was never wrong. The order was.

The business case prices the build, not the change

The number that goes into the business case is usually a finger in the air, anchored on something vaguely similar that was delivered before. Set aside whether the estimate is accurate, the bigger problem is what it covers. It prices what needs delivering. It does not price the cost of change.

When a capability changes, the process around it changes, the data it depends on has to be fit for the new use, the roles that operate it need to be redefined and retrained, the controls sitting over it need to be reviewed, and the operations team has to absorb a period where the old way and the new way both exist. None of that appears in a delivery estimate, and all of it is real cost that lands on someone's budget later.

A capability view makes those consequences visible before the money is committed. That is the point at which a roadmap becomes credible to a finance director rather than merely approved by one.

What business architecture actually produces

Leaving aside the softer aspects of the discipline, there are two deliverables that matter.

The first is the capability model. It describes what the organisation does, independent of who currently does it. Onboard a customer. Price a product. Collect a payment. Resolve a complaint. It is deliberately stable, because what an organisation does changes far more slowly than how it is arranged to do it.

The second is the value stream. It describes how value actually reaches the customer, moving across capabilities and across departmental boundaries. This is the view that answers the operations person in the kick-off meeting. Repeated workaround requests are almost never a single capability being weak. They are a value stream with a gap in it, where two functions each did their part correctly and the customer still did not get what they needed.

Between them, these two views give the room something to point at. The argument stops being between directors and becomes an argument about which capability is weakest against the strategy the board has already signed off. That is a much easier argument to settle, and it gives an executive a defensible basis for saying no, which is usually the thing they lack.

Capabilities outlive the org chart

Organisation structures change frequently. In most firms, more frequently than a multi-year strategy takes to deliver. A roadmap anchored to departments has to be re-cut every time, and each re-cut loses information and momentum.

The capabilities themselves do not move. The organisation still onboards customers and collects payments after the reshuffle, whatever the new boxes are called. A plan expressed in capabilities survives a restructure, a change of leadership, and a change of supplier. That is not a theoretical benefit for any firm carrying a three-year strategy through two reorganisations.

The line between requirement and solution

One of the hardest things to hold in any project is the line between what is needed and how it will be done. It has always been blurred. It is worse now, because a working prototype takes an afternoon, and once a demo exists in the room it stops being one option and starts being the plan.

Business architecture keeps the requirement stated at the level of the capability, independent of the solution. Once the capability is genuinely understood, several things can satisfy it. Someone keying it in manually. An RPA robot. A new piece of software. Outsourcing it. A plaster in the form of an AI tool while a longer-term answer is worked out. Those five options differ enormously in cost, permanence and risk, and they are all valid answers to the same requirement. The choice between them is a business decision that should be made deliberately, with the trade-offs visible.

Go back to document generation. The capability is the same whichever way it is met. Someone can key the letters by hand, which costs nothing to set up and does not scale. A Word macro can populate a template from a spreadsheet, which one person can build in a week and one person will then own for the next five years. A third-party document product does the job properly, at a licence cost and an integration effort that has to be justified. An AI tool can draft the letter from the customer record, which is quick to stand up and moves the question to how the output is checked before it reaches a customer.

Those are four different answers to one requirement, and the right one depends on volume, on how often the templates change, and on how much regulatory exposure sits in the output. That is a business decision. It is not a decision that should be made by whoever built the demo first.

This is also where automation proposals should be tested. An automation case arrives as a tool decision. The prior question is which capability is being changed, and whether anyone has written down how that capability actually runs, including the exceptions. If that knowledge only exists in the heads of the people currently doing the work, the tool is not the constraint.

Analysis is not architecture

Business analysts have historically absorbed much of this work, and the two are often treated as interchangeable. They are not, and it is worth being clear about the difference, because a firm that has only one of them has a gap it may not have noticed.

Analysis, as the name suggests, goes deep. It understands in detail how something works, elicits what is needed, and builds the bridge between that and the technology within a given project. It is demanding work and good analysts are hard to find.

Architecture goes wide. It looks across the organisation rather than within a project, and its unit of interest is the capability rather than the requirement. A firm with strong analysis and no architecture delivers each project competently and still cannot sequence a portfolio, because nothing is looking at the dependencies between projects or at what the whole set does to the operating model. Which is how you end up back in the room this article started in.

What this is not

Business architecture has a reputation problem, and it is largely deserved. Plenty of teams have produced large model estates that nobody read, maintained by a central function that became a checkpoint rather than a help. If that is what the term brings to mind, the scepticism is reasonable.

The useful version is much smaller. It is a capability model at two levels of depth, assessed against the strategy the organisation has already stated, with the value streams that matter most mapped across it. That is a matter of weeks, not quarters, and it is enough to sequence a roadmap and to price a change honestly. Anything beyond that should be earned by demand, not produced on principle.

Look at your current roadmap. Is it a list of capabilities the organisation needs to build, or a list of things it has decided to buy?
