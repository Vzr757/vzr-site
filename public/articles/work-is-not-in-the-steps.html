The work is not in the steps

A document can be accurate, followed exactly, and still produce the wrong result. The steps are the residue of the work.

Published
August 2026
Reading time
5 minutes
Topic
Judgement, not procedure

Every business has that one person. Some have two or three. When they are on holiday, certain things wait. Nobody decided this and nobody has written it down, but everyone knows which things they are, and the week quietly reshapes itself around their absence.

At some point they were asked to document it. They were happy to do it, they took it seriously. What came back was a proper document: steps in order, with the system names and the right screens. It was accurate. Then a new joiner followed it exactly, but still got it wrong.

The first reaction is that the document was not detailed enough. So the next version is longer, and the same thing happens.

Consider a sourdough recipe. Every step is written down and you follow every one of them, in order, to the gram. You even know the finger press test, because that is in there too. And you still take a brick out of the oven.

The steps were never the hard part. The dough responds to how lively the starter was that week, how warm the kitchen is, what the flour has absorbed. Someone who has made it fifty times reads all of that without stopping to think about it. They are not following the recipe better than you are. The finger press test is written down. What counts as ready is not.

The problem is not the level of detail. The document records the wrong layer.

Think about what that person actually does across a day. They are making judgements, one after another, most of them small enough that they never register as judgements at all. Whether this one gets chased today or on Friday. Whether a figure looks wrong before anything has flagged it. Which of the three ways you handle this applies to this particular client. The steps are the residue of the work.

That is also why the person cannot help you fix it. They do not experience the judgement as knowledge. They experience it as obvious. Ask them what they know and they will give you the part they can see, which is the steps. The rest does not feel like information to them. It feels like noticing.

The material you want is the exceptions

Not the run that went through cleanly, but the one where they stopped, looked again, and did something different. Asking someone how they do their job produces the steps again, because that is the part they can see. Asking about the last five times they nearly did something different gets you closer. Each of those has a condition attached, and it can be written down: this is what I saw, this is the call I made, this is why. Three lines, and worth more than another page of procedure.

This does not run forever. After a few weeks the exceptions stop being new, and the same handful of judgements start arriving in different clothes. The list stops growing. That is the difference between this and documenting steps, where there is always another screen to describe.

Steps still get recorded, but notice what they usually contain. Anyone recruited into the role already knows the basics, the same way they already know how to bake with instant yeast. What they do not know is what to do when the kitchen is five degrees warmer than usual, when to stop, and when to escalate. There are things that are genuinely not obvious, and things that someone needs to execute without applying judgement, and those should be written down properly. But they come second, and they go stale slowly. The decisions are the part that carries.

The reason to bother

The reason to bother is not what happens if the person leaves, though that is the version most owners have in mind, filed under things to sort out eventually. The cost is being paid now. Work queues behind one person, and the queue never appears as a failure, because nothing fails. It simply moves at the speed of one person's attention. The person at the front of it rarely enjoys the position as much as everyone assumes, either. Being interrupted all day with questions only you can answer leaves you the least able of anyone to do the work you were hired for. Hiring does not resolve it either, at least not at first, because the new person can only learn this by sitting beside the person who has it, which spends exactly the capacity the hire was meant to add.

The test of whether you have captured any of it is not whether a document exists. It is whether a second person can make the call.
