The division that is no longer geographic

Global Capability Centres are the newest way to divide labour by geography. But the division being settled now is not geographic at all — it is between people and machines.

Published
July 2026
Reading time
9 minutes
Topic
Sourcing & the division of labour

There are only a handful of ways to organise work that an organisation cannot or will not do itself.

You can hand a defined project to an offshore or nearshore firm. You can enter a longer partnership with one. You can hire individual contractors on a day rate and keep them close. Or you can set up a capability centre: your own entity, in another country, staffed with your own people.

The Global Capability Centre is the newest of these, not the first. It is worth being precise about what it changes.

What a capability centre actually changes

The earlier models are scoped. A partner delivers a project. A contractor fills a gap. Accountability is bounded by the piece of work.

A capability centre takes a domain. It staffs every expertise that domain requires and owns the outcome rather than the task. That is a genuine shift in how accountability is drawn, and it is the reason the model attracts attention.

The benefit is price, not supply

The main case for a capability centre is talent access. In the UK that case is weak. There is no shortage of capable technical people here. There is a shortage of organisations willing to pay two or three times the offshore rate for them.

That is a price problem, not a supply problem, and it is worth separating the two because every earlier model already answered it. Project outsourcing was labour arbitrage. Offshore partnership was labour arbitrage. The capability centre is labour arbitrage with a different accountability wrapper around it.

That is not a criticism. It is a correction to the framing. If the benefit is cost, speed to a standing team, and reduced hiring risk, then the model should be assessed on cost, speed and hiring risk, not on access to skills that were available at home all along.

Autonomy is the claim that does not survive contact

The promise attached to domain ownership is autonomy. The team owns the domain, so the team decides.

In practice, nobody hands an offshore team unreviewed control of a finance system. The work still passes through peer review, approval and whatever governance the parent already runs. That is not the problem. The London team has the same checkpoints.

The problem is what a checkpoint costs when the reviewer and the doer do not share context. When they do, review is quick, because most of what needs to be understood is already understood. When they do not, every checkpoint becomes a translation exercise. The cost is not one delay. It compounds across every handoff, and it is rarely modelled when the business case is written.

The work that travels is the work that needs context most

There is a pattern in what gets moved first. Support. Business as usual. Remediation. The long tail of things that never made it into a project.

That work is handed over because it looks low value. But it is exception heavy, and exceptions are precisely where the undocumented knowledge lives. The regulatory carve-out nobody wrote down. The workaround that exists because of one client relationship. The step that only makes sense if you know what happened in 2009.

So the standard model sends the most context-hungry work to the place with the least context, and then explains the resulting friction as a maturity problem that time will solve.

What the pattern suggests

The industry figures I have seen presented recently make a consistent point. Assisted models remain the majority, where the centre supports the parent rather than taking ownership of anything outright. What has shifted over the past decade is the arrangement around them: away from joint ventures, toward build, operate and transfer.

Read one way, that is organisations buying optionality. Get support without commitment. If it works, take it over. If it does not, walk away.

That is a reasonable thing to want. It is also worth being honest about what it costs.

What optionality costs

Contractual IP in a capability centre is settled. The entity belongs to the parent and work product vests there. That is not the exposure.

The exposure is know-how. Over three or four years an arrangement accumulates a great deal of understanding about how the organisation actually works, and very little of it is written anywhere that survives the arrangement ending. When the handover finally comes, what moves across is people, systems and contracts. Whether understanding moves with them is a separate question, and usually an unasked one.

The gap does not appear immediately. It appears years later, when someone has to reconstruct why a process works the way it does and there is nobody left who knows.

The condition for it working, and the bind

The standard prescription is representation. Put people from the parent inside the centre, holding real decision rights, taking active part in adoption rather than visiting quarterly. That is correct. Culture and organisational context do not transfer through training, stand-ups or offsites, and expecting an offshore team to absorb them without exposure is unfair to that team.

The difficulty is who qualifies. Effective representation requires people who hold the tacit knowledge and carry decision authority. Those are the same people the parent can least afford to release, and they are already the busiest people in the building.

Which puts the scarce resource onshore, not offshore.

What has not moved is what sits in people's heads.

What AI changes, and what it does not

Technical knowledge and design are becoming cheap quickly. Reading a codebase, tracing data flows, reconstructing a process map from what a system does rather than what its documentation claims: all of that is now within reach and improving fast.

What has not moved is what sits in people's heads.

An AI observing an experienced person at work can learn a great deal, and it can probably absorb most of the routine. But it learns the part that had already become procedural through repetition. What it misses is judgement under conditions it has not seen, and those conditions are rare by definition, which means observation has the fewest examples exactly where the difficulty is greatest.

So the constraint has not disappeared. It has moved and narrowed. The remaining portion is smaller and considerably harder, and it is the portion that decides whether any of this works.

There is a further question that rarely gets asked. Judgement of this kind is not taught. It is built by doing the routine work for years, seeing enough ordinary cases to recognise the moment one stops being ordinary. That routine work is what junior people are given. Doing it for years is how they become the senior ones. If a machine takes it, the organisation keeps the experts it already has and quietly removes the ground that produced them. The exceptions keep arriving. In twenty years there is nobody who has seen enough ordinary cases to recognise an unusual one.

The incentive problem, said plainly

There is a reason knowledge stays where it is.

If your standing in an organisation comes from being the person who knows how something works, you have a rational interest in remaining that person. This is not obstruction and it is not generational. It is what the structure rewards. Anyone who has run an automation programme has met it, usually described afterwards as a culture issue.

The technical side of a programme is rarely unaware of this. It simply has no mechanism to reach it. Meanwhile the people who hold the knowledge have no reason to surrender it. Both positions are rational, and the programme fails between them.

The way out is not persuasion. It is making the knowledge holder more valuable rather than less. Someone who knows how the work is really done, equipped to direct an automated system and to handle what falls outside it, occupies a stronger position than they did before, not a weaker one. That has to be true rather than reassuring, and it means being specific about who it applies to. Anyone who has been through a transformation programme has heard the comforting version and discounted it.

A new division of labour

None of this makes the capability centre a bad model. For commodity volume work with a low exception rate, the case holds and the arbitrage is real.

But it is an answer to an older question. The division of labour has always been the underlying problem: who does which part of the work, and how the parts are put back together afterwards. Project outsourcing, partnership, contracting and the capability centre are all answers of the same type. They divide labour by geography.

The division being settled now is not geographic. It is between people and machines.

And the test turns out to be the same one. Work with a low exception rate and little dependency on context can be relocated. It is also the work a machine can absorb. Work that is mostly exceptions can be neither, because in both cases the work is being separated from the thing it depends on.

That is worth sitting with. The work best suited to a capability centre is the work most likely to be automated within the life of the arrangement. Build a centre around it and you may be housing something that will not need housing.

For everything else, the better answer is often to keep the work, keep the people, and invest in the tooling and the extraction. It costs less than it appears to, and the know-how stays where it can still be used.

Which returns to the part that is genuinely difficult. Getting what people know onto the table, in a form that outlasts them and that a machine can act on, and lowering the approval barrier enough that a team can actually operate.

That is not a sourcing question. It never was.
