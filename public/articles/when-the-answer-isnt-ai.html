When the answer isn't AI

Every automation decision is really a question about where judgement belongs. Answer it honestly and the right tool is often smaller, cheaper, and duller than the pitch.

Published
July 2026
Reading time
4 minutes
Topic
Right-sized automation

Most automation projects begin with a technology looking for a home. The better ones begin with a process nobody fully understands — and end with a decision about how much of it a machine should be trusted to make.

We are asked, often, to "add AI" to a workflow. It is rarely the right framing. A tool is not a strategy, and the interesting question is never can we automate this. Almost anything can be automated. The question is where human judgement genuinely earns its keep, and what it costs to move that judgement into software.

Get that wrong in the ambitious direction and you build something impressive that no one trusts. Get it wrong in the cautious direction and you leave real value on the table. Getting it right means being honest, early, about a process most organisations have never actually written down.

Right-sizing is a design decision, not a budget one

"Right-sized" is often heard as a polite word for cheap. It isn't. Right-sizing is the deliberate match between the difficulty of a task and the machinery you point at it. A task with clear inputs, stable rules, and a low cost of error wants a rule. A task thick with exceptions, context, and consequence wants a person — or a person with a machine handing them a head start.

The failure mode we see most is a heavyweight platform bolted onto a lightweight problem: months of build, a licence in perpetuity, and a process now more fragile than the spreadsheet it replaced. The opposite failure is quieter but just as expensive — a genuinely hard, high-volume judgement left entirely to people because "AI felt risky", when a well-placed model would have carried the load and left the judgement where it belonged.

The goal is not the most automation. It is the least machinery that makes the outcome reliable.

Where AI actually earns its place

When we do reach for AI, we try to reach for it narrowly. On a recent grant-appeals engagement the whole process was rebuilt from standard tooling already sitting in the organisation — no new licence, no new training. AI did exactly one thing: it summarised the accumulated feedback at the final decision point, so an approver could read a clear picture instead of wading through every comment.

That is the shape of a good AI decision. It sits at a single, well-chosen step. Its job is bounded. A person can see what it did and overrule it. And if it were removed tomorrow, the process would still run — just a little slower. AI as a resource inside a governed process, not the process itself.

A short test before you automate

Three questions decide most of it:

  • What does the work actually involve? Not the tidy version in the process map — the real one, exceptions and all. You cannot right-size a task you have only seen from a distance.
  • Where is the judgement, and what does a wrong call cost? High-consequence judgement stays close to a human, with the machine assisting rather than deciding.
  • What is the simplest thing that would work? Start there. Reach for something heavier only when the simple thing demonstrably falls short.

Proving it before committing

None of this has to be settled by argument. It is cheaper to find out. Understanding a single process properly — and proving the right fix on it before committing to a wider programme — turns a debate about technology into a demonstration. Sometimes the answer that survives that test is AI. Sometimes it is a form, a rule, and a tool the team already owns.

Either way, the discipline is the same: let the work decide the machinery, not the other way around.
